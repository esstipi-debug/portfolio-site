Case Study — Safety Stock & EOQ

Right-Sizing Safety Stock, Reorder Points & Order Quantities

The two most common — and most often misapplied — inventory formulas, worked end to end from raw demand statistics to a defensible policy.

Problem

Two questions come up on almost every inventory engagement: how much should I order at once, and how much buffer should I hold against demand variability? Get the order quantity wrong and you pay too much in ordering or holding costs; get the safety stock wrong and you either stock out or tie up working capital that didn't need to be there.

Method

The Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) model balances ordering cost against holding cost to find the order size that minimizes total annual cost (Vandeput 2020, §2.2.4). Safety stock is sized from the standard deviation of demand and a target cycle service level, converted to a buffer via the service-level z-factor (Vandeput 2020, Table 4.1) — not a flat percentage of average demand, which is the most common mistake I see.

What I built

I ran both formulas end to end through Kern's eoq and safety_stock modules on a representative demand profile, then combined them into a reorder point: expected demand during lead time plus the safety-stock buffer.

Result

239EOQ order quantity (units), D=1,000/yr, k=€50/order, h=€1.75/unit/yr
€418Minimum total annual cost at that order quantity
41Safety stock (units), μ=100, σ=25, 95% service level
141Resulting reorder point (units)

Worked examples reproduced from Kern's engine (src/eoq.py, src/safety_stock.py) — and the arithmetic can be checked with a calculator: EOQ = √(2 × 1,000 × 50 / 1.75) = 239, and safety stock = 1.65 × 25 = 41.

How this compares

Two common shortcuts, measured against the exact same scenario as the worked example above.

79%Service level actually achieved by the common "20% of demand" safety-stock rule of thumb — against a 95% target
29%More expensive per year from ordering a round lot of 500 instead of the EOQ-optimal 239 units

Both are real outputs of the engine, not illustrations, and both can be checked by hand from the scenario above: 20 units of buffer is a z of 0.8, and a lot of 500 costs €538 a year against €418.
