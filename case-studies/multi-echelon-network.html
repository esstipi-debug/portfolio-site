Case Study — Network Design

Where should safety stock sit in a multi-stage supply chain?

Not at every stage. A buffer at the supplier, another at the distribution centre and another at the store covers the same demand variability two or three times over. The Guaranteed-Service Model (Graves and Willems; Vandeput 2020, chapter 10) sets each stage’s service time so the whole chain holds the least stock that still meets the end-to-end service level. The usual result is large buffers at a few stages and none at the others.

Worked example

A three-stage serial chain: supplier, distribution centre, store. Stage lead times of 4, 3 and 2 days. Demand of 100 units a day with a standard deviation of 25. Review periods of 1, 2 and 4 days. Target: 95% end to end.

The honest reading of that number

6.7% on a three-stage textbook chain is not a headline, and I would not quote it to you as a saving. The point of the example is the zero in the middle: the optimiser removed an entire stage’s buffer. On a real network with more stages and more nodes, that structural result is where the money is. What it is worth on yours is something I would measure, not promise.

Where this doesn’t work

Perishable goods. Moving a buffer upstream to save holding cost can mean stock that ages before it reaches the shelf. The engine refuses to run buffer sizing for a food client without shelf life in the model, and asks for it instead — because buffer sizing with no notion of shelf life turns capital into spoilage. Stages that miss their promised service time. The model assumes each stage keeps the service time it quotes. If the distribution centre routinely runs late, the stage the model left without a buffer becomes the one that stocks out. Distribution networks. One warehouse feeding many stores needs the tree version of the model, not the serial one shown here.
